Week 2

Minimum spanning tree — Prim's algorithm

The cheapest set of edges that still reaches every node. Grow one tree outward, always taking the cheapest edge that leaves the tree.

The trace, starting from A

  • frontier A–B 4, A–C 2take A–C (2)
  • A–B 4, B–C 1, C–D 8, C–E 7take B–C (1)
  • B–D 5, C–D 8, C–E 7take B–D (5)
  • C–E 7, D–E 2, D–F 6take D–E (2)
  • C–E 7, D–F 6, E–F 3take E–F (3)
  • total weight2+1+5+2+3 = 13
edges in any spanning tree = n − 1 = 5 Start anywhere: starting from C gives the same five edges in a different order, still 13. With all-distinct weights the MST is unique.

First-timer traps

  • Grabbing the globally cheapest unused edge. At step 3 the cheapest edge left in the whole graph is D–E (2), but neither D nor E is in the tree yet, so Prim cannot take it. Only edges with exactly one endpoint inside the tree are candidates. Taking the global minimum is Kruskal's rule — a different algorithm that needs a cycle check.
  • Adding an edge whose endpoints are both already in the tree. That closes a cycle, and a tree has none. After each addition, exactly one new node joins.
  • Losing count. An MST on n nodes has exactly n−1 edges. Five edges here — if you finish with four you left a node stranded, with six you made a cycle.
  • Assuming the MST contains shortest paths. It does not. C to E costs 7 by the direct edge, but 1+5+2 = 8 along the tree. MST minimises total weight, never pairwise distance.
  • Panicking at ties. Equal-weight edges mean several valid MSTs, all with the same total. Your answer isn't wrong for differing from the book's — but say which tie you broke.

Sanity check: add the chosen weights, then swap any one tree edge for a dashed one. If the total ever drops, your tree isn't minimal.

Week 3

Degree centrality

Count the edges at a node. The cheapest measure there is, and the one that most often answers a different question than the one you asked.

How it is calculated

Cd(vi) = di Directed: din is prestige, dout is gregariousness.
  • A, B, D, F, G2 each
  • C, E3 each
  • Σ di16 = 2m ✓
normalised: din−1 = 36 = 0.50 for C and E Other conventions: di/maxj dj (= 1.00 for C) and di/2m (= 0.188 for C).

First-timer traps

  • Reading an edge list and counting each edge once. Edge A–B raises the degree of A and of B. The check that catches it every time: Σdi must equal 2m — here 16 for 8 edges. An odd total is always an error.
  • Dividing by n instead of n−1. A node cannot connect to itself, so the largest possible degree is n−1 = 6, not 7.
  • Mixing normalisations inside one comparison. C scores 0.50, 1.00 or 0.188 depending on the denominator. All three are "degree centrality". Pick one and label it.
  • Reporting total degree on a directed graph. "Who is most influential?" normally means in-degree. Adding in and out hides the difference between a celebrity and a spammer.
  • Self-loops. A self-loop adds 2 to an undirected degree, not 1.
  • Believing the answer. Degree cannot see position. Here it ranks the graph's single broker joint-last.

Eigenvector centrality

You are important if your neighbours are important. Defined circularly, and resolved by iterating until the circle stops changing.

The power method, from all-ones

ce(vi) = 1λ Σj Aj,i ce(vj)  ⇔  λCe = ATCe

λ1 = 2.3429 t = 1 is just the degree vector — eigenvector centrality is degree, iterated. At t = 2, D ties C and E; stop there and you get the ranking wrong.

First-timer traps

  • Stopping the iteration too early. Two steps in, this graph says C = D = E. Three steps in, the order is right. Iterate until the ranking stops moving, not until you are bored.
  • Comparing raw values across tools. The eigenvector is only defined up to scale. Unit length gives C = 0.4496; scaled to max = 1 it is 1.00; scaled to sum = 1 it is different again. Only ranks and ratios are meaningful.
  • Not normalising between iterations. The numbers grow by a factor of λ each step. On a real graph they overflow; on paper they get unmanageable fast.
  • Taking the wrong eigenvalue. You need the largest one. The others produce vectors with mixed signs, which cannot be centralities — negative "importance" is the tell.
  • Using A instead of AT on a directed graph. That measures who you point at rather than who points at you — the exact reverse of prestige.
  • Running it on a disconnected graph. Perron–Frobenius needs connectedness. Otherwise the whole vector can pile onto one component and hand every other node a flat zero.
Week 3

Katz centrality

Eigenvector centrality plus a flat allowance β, so no node is condemned to zero just because nobody points at it. Needs a second graph to show the problem it solves.

How it is calculated

CKatz = α AT CKatz + β·1 closed form: CKatz = β (I − α AT)−1 · 1
  • λmax(AT)1.3247
  • so α must be <1/λ = 0.755
  • with α = 0.25, β = 1
iterP1P2P3P4
t = 11.001.001.001.00
t = 21.751.251.501.00
t = 31.941.441.561.00
settles2.031.511.631.00

P4 now scores 1.00 = β instead of 0. And the ranking of P2 and P3 flips against eigenvector centrality: P3 gains from P4's free allowance, P2 does not.

First-timer traps

  • Borrowing α = 0.85 from PageRank. Katz needs α < 1/λmax, which is 0.755 here. At 0.85 the matrix I − αAT is no longer invertible in the useful sense and the iteration runs away to infinity. Compute λmax first, every time; there is no universal safe α.
  • Expecting β to change the ranking. It cannot. β multiplies the whole vector, so it rescales every score identically. Only α changes who wins — tune α, report β.
  • Forgetting what the two extremes mean. α = 0 gives every node exactly β (all structure discarded); α approaching 1/λ drowns β and reproduces eigenvector centrality. Katz is the dial between them.
  • Transposing the wrong way. With AT, centrality flows along incoming edges. Use A and you rank pages by who they link to.
  • Reading absolute values as meaningful. P1 = 2.03 is not "twice as central" in any absolute sense; it is 2.03 units on a scale you set with β.
Week 3

PageRank

Katz hands your full score to every node you point at. PageRank divides it by your out-degree first, so influence is spent, not copied.

How it is calculated

Cp(vi) = α Σj Aj,i Cp(vj)djout + β closed form: Cp = β (I − α ATD−1)−1 · 1, with D = diag(dout)
iterP1P2P3P4
t = 1.463.250.250.038
t = 2.372.431.160.038
t = 4.405.354.204.038
settles.387.366.209.038
λmax(ATD−1) = 1 exactly True for every graph with no dangling node, so the α < 1/λ rule reduces to the familiar α < 1. Undirected graphs: always λ = 1.

First-timer traps

  • Dangling nodes. A node with out-degree 0 makes D−1 undefined — a divide-by-zero that silently leaks rank out of the system. Real link graphs are full of them. Patch it before you run: send a dangling node's score to all nodes uniformly.
  • Dividing by in-degree. The division is by the sender's out-degree, djout. Using in-degree inverts the whole idea.
  • Calling the closed-form output a probability. With β = 1 the raw scores here are 10.32, 9.77, 5.58, 1.00 and sum to 26.67. Divide by the total before you say "38.7% of the surfer's time".
  • Stopping at two iterations. Look at the table: at t = 1, P2 and P3 are tied; at t = 2, P2 is ahead of P1. Both rankings are wrong. The values oscillate before they settle.
  • Getting the matrix orientation backwards. ATD−1 must be column-stochastic — each column summing to 1. If your rows sum to 1 instead, you have transposed it and are ranking the wrong direction.
  • Treating α = 0.85 as fixed by theory. It is Google's published convention. Lower α means more teleporting, flatter scores, faster convergence.

Sanity check: after normalising, the scores must sum to 1 at every iteration. If the total drifts, you have a dangling node.

Week 4

Betweenness centrality

Not how many people you know — how many pairs of people must go through you. The broker's measure.

How it is calculated

Cb(vi) = Σs≠t≠vi σst(vi)σst σst = number of shortest s–t paths; σst(vi) = how many of them pass through vi.
  • D: pairs {A,B,C} × {E,F,G}3 × 3 = 9
  • each has one shortest pathall through D
  • Cb(D)9
  • C: pairs {A,B} × {D,E,F,G}2 × 4 = 8
  • Cb(C)8
normalised: 9(n−1)(n−2)/2 = 915 = 0.60

First-timer traps

  • Counting every pair twice. Undirected, the pair (A, E) is the same trip as (E, A). Count unordered pairs and divide by (n−1)(n−2)/2 = 15; count ordered pairs and divide by (n−1)(n−2) = 30. Mixing the two is why your answer is exactly double the book's.
  • Including the endpoints. The condition is s ≠ t ≠ vi. A node is never "between" itself and someone else, so C never gets credit for the pair (C, D).
  • Ignoring path ties. When two shortest paths run between s and t and your node is on one, it earns ½, not 1. This graph has no ties, which makes it a poor place to practise the habit — but on the karate club it decides the ranking.
  • Counting all paths instead of shortest ones. Only geodesics count. A longer route through a node earns nothing.
  • Expecting degree to predict it. D has the minimum degree and the maximum betweenness. That gap is the finding — it names the node whose removal splits the network.
  • Disconnected graphs. Pairs with no path between them contribute nothing; just skip them rather than treating the distance as infinite in the numerator.
Week 4

Closeness centrality

How few hops it takes you to reach everyone else. The inverse of average distance, so bigger is closer.

How it is calculated

Cc(vi) = 1l̄vi,   l̄vi = 1n−1 Σj≠i li,j
nodeΣ distancesaverageCc
A, B, F, G152.500.400
C, E111.830.545
D101.670.600
D: 2+2+1+1+2+2 = 10 over 6 others hops from D to A, B, C, E, F, G respectively

First-timer traps

  • Reporting the average distance and calling it closeness. Closeness is its reciprocal. D's average is 1.67 and its closeness is 0.60 — quote the wrong one and your ranking silently inverts.
  • Dividing by n. There are n−1 = 6 other nodes. Including yourself adds a zero to the numerator and shrinks every score by the same wrong factor.
  • Disconnected graphs. One unreachable node makes the sum infinite and the closeness 0 for everybody in that component — one isolate destroys the measure. Use harmonic closeness (the mean of 1/l instead of 1/mean l) when the graph may be disconnected.
  • Directed graphs. Distance is not symmetric, so out-closeness (who you can reach) and in-closeness (who can reach you) are different measures with different rankings. Say which one you computed.
  • Over-reading small gaps. The whole graph fits in 0.40–0.60. Closeness saturates because distances grow logarithmically in real networks — a 0.02 difference is usually noise.
Week 4

Clustering coefficient & transitivity

Are your friends friends with each other? Two standard answers to that question, and they do not agree.

Both versions, same graph

local   C(vi) = connected pairs of neighboursdi(di−1)/2
  • A, B, F, G1/1 = 1.00
  • C, E1/3 = 0.33
  • D (neighbours C, E unlinked)0/1 = 0.00
  • average local C0.667
global   C = 3 × trianglespairs of neighbours = 3 × 211 = 0.545

Two triangles (ABC, EFG); Σ di(di−1)/2 = 11. 0.667 ≠ 0.545 on the same graph.

First-timer traps

  • Assuming the two agree. Average local C (0.667) weights every node equally; global C (0.545) weights every triangle equally, so hubs dominate it. In NetworkX they are average_clustering and transitivity. Name which one you used.
  • The 3× versus 6× confusion. Both are right: 3 × triangles over unordered neighbour pairs (11 here), or 6 × triangles over ordered length-2 paths (22 here). Both give 0.545. Mixing a 6 with an unordered denominator gives 1.09 — and C can never exceed 1.
  • Degree-1 nodes. The denominator d(d−1)/2 is zero, so local C is undefined. Most software quietly records 0, which drags the average down. In a star graph that is the difference between "no clustering" and "not applicable".
  • Writing di2 as the denominator. It is the number of pairs, di(di−1)/2 — 3 for C, not 9.
  • Running it on a directed graph as-is. The formula above assumes undirected. Decide whether to symmetrise first or use a directed variant; do not just feed it a directed adjacency matrix.
Week 4

Reciprocity

Of the arrows you send, how many come back? Transitivity's two-node cousin, and directed-only.

How it is calculated

R = Σi<j Ai,jAj,i|E|/2 = 1m Tr(A2)
  • mutual pairs (P1, P2)1
  • |E|/2 = 6/23
  • Tr(A2)2
  • R = 1/3 = 2/60.333
expected R under randomness = density = 64×3 = 0.50 0.333 < 0.50 — these pages reciprocate less than chance would predict.

First-timer traps

  • The two formulas look like they disagree. They don't. Σi<j counts each mutual pair once (1) over |E|/2 = 3; Tr(A²) counts it from both ends (2) over m = 6. Both give ⅓. Trouble starts when you pair one numerator with the other denominator and land on 0.167 or 0.667.
  • Calling m the number of node pairs. Here m = 6 directed edges, not 12 possible ones and not 3 undirected ones.
  • Computing it on an undirected graph. Every undirected edge is trivially mutual, so R = 1 and the measure says nothing. It only has meaning when direction is real.
  • Reading a high R as strong mutuality. Dense graphs reciprocate by accident. Compare against the density baseline — 0.333 against an expected 0.50 means this graph is less reciprocal than random, the opposite of the naive read.
  • Forgetting to zero the diagonal. Self-loops land on the diagonal of A² and inflate the trace.

Similarity — Jaccard, cosine, Pearson

Two nodes are alike if they keep the same company. Structural equivalence compares neighbourhoods, not positions.

How they are calculated

σJaccard = |N(vi) ∩ N(vj)||N(vi) ∪ N(vj)| = 13
σcosine = |N(vi) ∩ N(vj)|√(|N(vi)|·|N(vj)|) = 1√4 = 0.5
σPearson = correlation of rows Ai and Aj N(A) = {B, C}, N(B) = {A, C} → ∩ = {C}, ∪ = {A, B, C}

First-timer traps

  • Believing a single number. A–B (triangle partners) and A–D (facing each other across the bridge) get identical scores on all three metrics. Neighbourhood overlap cannot tell a cohesive pair from a structurally analogous one — that is a limitation of the definition, not a mistake in your arithmetic.
  • Forgetting that adjacency doesn't count. N(v) excludes v itself, so two nodes joined by an edge but sharing no neighbour score 0. If that reads wrong for your application, add each node to its own neighbourhood — and say so.
  • Comparing a Jaccard against a cosine. The same overlap is 0.333 or 0.500 depending only on which formula you used. Cosine always reads higher because it doesn't penalise the union.
  • Treating a negative Pearson as zero similarity. C and E score −0.167: they avoid each other's neighbourhoods more than chance predicts. Jaccard and cosine cannot express that; they both floor at 0.
  • Inconsistent diagonals. Pearson over adjacency rows includes Aii = 0. Whether you keep, drop or set those to 1 changes the number — fix one convention across every pair you compare.
Week 5

Modularity

How many more edges fall inside your proposed communities than would fall there by chance, given the same degrees. The score every community-detection method is trying to maximise.

How it is calculated

Q = 12m ΣiΣj ( Aij − didj2m ) δ(Pi, Pj) per community: Qc = (internal edges / m) − (Σ degrees / 2m)²
  • {A,B,C}: 3 internal, Σd = 73/8 − (7/16)²
  • = 0.3750 − 0.1914
  • {D,E,F,G}: 4 internal, Σd = 94/8 − (9/16)²
  • = 0.5000 − 0.3164
  • Q0.3672

Every partition of this graph

partitionQ
{ABC} | {DEFG}0.3672

everything in one0.0000

Two different partitions tie for the maximum, and they disagree about where D belongs. Nothing in the objective function can break that tie.

First-timer traps

  • Treating the maximum as "the" community structure. This is modularity's degeneracy problem, visible even at n = 7: many very different partitions sit at or near the peak. Report the ambiguity instead of the winner.
  • Being impressed by Q > 0. Putting the whole graph in one community always gives exactly 0, so beating zero is close to free. Real community structure usually means Q above roughly 0.3.
  • Comparing Q across graphs. Q depends on size and density, so "our network scored 0.41, theirs 0.38" is not a claim about anything.
  • The resolution limit. Modularity cannot see communities smaller than about √(2m) edges — it will merge two genuinely separate small groups because splitting them lowers Q. Bigger graph, worse problem.
  • Losing a factor of 2. The double sum runs over ordered pairs, so ΣijAij = 2m = 16, not 8. Checking that Q comes out 0 for the one-community partition catches the error instantly.
  • Summing over all pairs. δ(Pi, Pj) kills every cross-community term — you only ever add up terms inside a community.
Week 5

The Louvain method

Modularity maximisation is NP-hard, so Louvain settles for greedy: move each node to whichever neighbouring community gains most, collapse the result, repeat.

One move, computed in full

ΔQ = [ Σin+2ki,in2m − (Σtot+ki2m)² ] − [ Σin2m − (Σtot2m)² − (ki2m)² ]

Start: all seven nodes alone, Q = −0.1484. Move A into {B}: ki = dA = 2, ki,in = 1, Σin = 0, Σtot = dB = 2, 2m = 16.

  • (0+2)/16 − (4/16)²0.1250 − 0.0625
  • minus [0 − (2/16)² − (2/16)²]+ 0.0313
  • ΔQ for A → {B}+0.0938
  • ΔQ for A → {C}+0.0781
  • new Q after the move−0.0547 ✓

A joins B, not C — the larger gain wins. Direct recomputation of Q agrees with the formula to the last digit, which is how you verify your ΔQ implementation.

First-timer traps

  • Computing ΔQ against stale communities. Σin and Σtot change after every accepted move. Evaluating a whole sweep against the starting assignment is the single most common Louvain bug.
  • Forgetting to remove the node first. The real gain is "take i out of its current community, then insert it into the candidate". Skipping the removal makes a node look good in a community it is already in and freezes the algorithm early.
  • Dropping self-loops in Phase 2. The self-loop is the community's internal density. Lose it and the second pass sees a sparse graph and merges everything.
  • Expecting one canonical answer. Louvain is order-dependent: a different node ordering can end at a different partition, usually with near-identical Q. Fix a seed, and run it several times before you name communities.
  • Stopping after Phase 1. One pass is a local optimum of one level. The method is defined by repeating both phases until Q stops improving — and the hierarchy it builds along the way is often the useful output.
  • Trusting the communities to be connected. Louvain can return a community that is internally disconnected. If that matters, use the Leiden algorithm, which guarantees it cannot happen.
Week 5

Girvan–Newman

The opposite strategy: instead of merging nodes, cut the edges that carry the most traffic between groups, and watch the network fall apart into communities.

The loop

  • 1. edge betweenness, all edges12, 12, 5, 5, 5, 5, 1, 1
  • 2. remove the maximumD–E
  • 3. recalculateC–D: 12 → 3
  • 4. repeatuntil no edges left

Removing D–E splits the graph into {A,B,C,D} and {E,F,G} — one of the two tied maximum-modularity partitions. Break the tie the other way and you get the other one.

Why it costs so much

O(m²n) overall Every single removal triggers a fresh all-pairs shortest-path computation across the whole graph.

Perfectly practical on a 34-node karate club, hopeless past a few thousand edges. That cost is exactly why Louvain exists.

First-timer traps

  • Not recalculating after every removal. The whole algorithm lives in this step. C–D scored 12 while D–E existed and 3 the moment it was gone. Remove the top-k edges from the original ranking in one go and you are running a different, wrong algorithm.
  • Using node betweenness. Girvan–Newman ranks edges. The two measures share a name and a formula shape and nothing else.
  • Expecting no ties. Symmetric graphs produce them constantly — two edges at 12 here. The tie-break is arbitrary and it changes the dendrogram, so record which edge you cut.
  • Looking for a stopping rule inside the algorithm. There isn't one: it runs until the graph has no edges. You choose the level to cut the dendrogram at, usually the one with the highest modularity.
  • Expecting balanced splits. Keep going and step 3 strips A–B, leaving A and B still attached through C. Late cuts produce singletons and slivers, not communities.
Week 6

TF-IDF and content-based recommendation

Turn each item's description into a weighted vector, turn the user into a vector of the same shape, and rank by the angle between them.

The corpus

wj,i = tfj,i × idfj,   idfj = log2 |D|docs containing j
termin docsidf = log₂(4/df)
social30.415
media, mining, data21.000
financial, market, network12.000

d1 social media mining · d2 social media data · d3 financial market data · d4 social network mining

  • cos(d1, d2)0.540
  • cos(d1, d4)0.350
  • cos(d1, d3)0.000
  • recommendd2, then d4
without idf, cos(d1,d2) = cos(d1,d4) = 0.667 Raw term presence cannot separate them at all. Weighting by rarity is what produces a ranking.

First-timer traps

  • Expecting the rarest shared term to win. d4 shares mining (idf 1.0) yet loses to d2. Its extra term network (idf 2.0) lengthens d4's vector from 1.47 to 2.27, and cosine divides by that length. Cosine rewards proportional overlap, not absolute overlap — items with lots of extra distinctive content are penalised.
  • Changing the log base and expecting the same numbers. The course uses log₂. Natural log or log₁₀ rescales every idf by a constant — which leaves the ranking identical but makes every value you report different from the book's.
  • Terms that appear in every document. idf = log(|D|/|D|) = 0, so the term vanishes completely. If your vocabulary is mostly common words, every similarity collapses to zero and the bug looks like a data problem.
  • Computing idf from the wrong corpus. df counts documents across the whole collection, not just the items one user touched. Recomputing idf per user makes scores incomparable between users.
  • Skipping the length normalisation. Drop the denominator and you are ranking by dot product, which simply prefers long descriptions.
  • Sanity bounds. With non-negative TF-IDF vectors, cosine lies in [0, 1]. A negative value or anything above 1 is an arithmetic error, every time.
  • Mistaking a zero for "dissimilar". d3 scores 0.000 because it shares no term with the profile — not because it was judged and rejected. Content-based filtering can never recommend it, however many similar users loved it. That is the cold-start-friendly, novelty-poor trade-off in one number.
